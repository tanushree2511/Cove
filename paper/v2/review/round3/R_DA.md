### Strongest Counter-Argument
Strip the paper of its framing and look at the numbers it measured. Without corrections, the clusterer is single linkage plus attachment plus a centroid merge. It ties average linkage within one point and is worse on low-res, mixed, profile and (with MBF) occluded photos. With corrections, three methods land within 0.0015 F1 of each other for the precise user: ours 0.9925, constrained average linkage 0.9914, and an Immich-style frozen rule 0.9928. The frozen rule is not worse on accuracy, is nearly order-stable (0.995), and is cheaper than anything here because it needs one matrix product per new face. The "100% of corrections hold" result is true by construction for all three. So what remains is (a) a proof that connected components of a threshold graph do not depend on insertion order, which is true of single linkage, and (b) the comparison with "re-apply", a baseline the authors designed. That baseline loses 27% of corrections for a user who clicks random faces, yet only 0.002 F1, and it beats the proposed method by 0.004 when the user makes a fallible mistake one time in ten, which is the only case in the study where users act like people. Add that "order dependence" is shown against a leader method the authors themselves wrote and shipped, and that 5.1 s versus 16.7 s compares against plain average linkage, not the method named. A reader learns that leader clustering depends on order, that stored constraints stay true, and that wrong constraints stay true too. None of this needed a new clusterer, and the paper's own best-supported advice (show corrections, let users undo them) is not implemented or tested.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths
1. **A simple stateful baseline:** persist cluster-level user actions (merge person A into B, remove face f from A) as sticky labels, then cluster only new faces and attach them. That is what a local-first photo app does in practice. It is close to the frozen rule, and the frozen rule ties ours, so the extra machinery has to justify itself by something else.
2. **Why re-apply loses:** the 27% failures are must-links blocked by the cannot-link guard (cannot-links always hold after step one). A different ordering or a fixed-point loop may remove most of them, so the 73% may measure the implementation rather than the idea.
3. **Why ours needs fewer corrections than avg-linkage sessions:** avg linkage leaves small errors in more libraries (224 vs 207), which a user would have to fix. If that is the real effect, then user effort, not post-hoc F1, is the better outcome measure, and it is not reported.
4. **Soft constraints:** a penalty or a confidence on each correction (decay if contradicted by later evidence) would address the sticky-wrong-correction result directly. The paper names the problem but only offers "show and undo".
5. **Cost explanation:** the small gap against average linkage (3x) mostly reflects an O(n^2) algorithm against a FAISS range search at one threshold. Any threshold-graph method (single linkage, DBSCAN) has the same cost profile, so the cost advantage does not come from the order-invariant or constraint design.

### Missing Stakeholder Perspectives
- A user who merges or splits people at the cluster level (the actual app action), not at random-face level.
- A user who wants to undo a correction, and a user who needs to see what corrections exist (the paper makes both necessary and neither is built).
- The maintainers of an app such as Immich, who would need a migration from the frozen model; the paper gives no cost of switching.
- The people in the photos (consent) are mentioned only in a closing sentence.

### Unexamined Premise
The paper assumes that the right outcome measure for corrections is whether a stored must-link or cannot-link stays true after re-clustering. For a user, the relevant outcome is probably how many clicks are needed to reach a satisfactory result over many imports, and whether the system ever undoes work the user already did. Neither is measured; "held" is a proxy chosen because the proposed method gets 100% on it by construction.

### Observations (Non-Defects)
- Round-2 DA items were fixed in substance: fair baselines, imperfect users, constrained-path timing and order-free contradiction handling. Table II reproduces the JSONs.
- The "Wrong corrections are sticky" paragraph is the most honest part of the paper, and it is a genuine finding even if the 10% is arbitrary.
- The 6-page limit and the table-heavy layout leave no room to add M1-M6 without cutting Sec. V-D to V-E; that trade-off is the authors' to make.
- My timing checks are single runs on a different machine; they establish that the repo's `c_avg` is much slower than scikit-learn's average linkage, not a precise ratio.
